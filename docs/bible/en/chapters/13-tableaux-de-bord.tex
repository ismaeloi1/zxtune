\chapter{Per-chip dashboards}\label{chap:dashboards}

The oscilloscope shows you the shape of the sound; the dashboard below it tells you how the chip makes it. We did not
want a single view full of registers: each chip family has its own, built around what makes its sound. On the SID it
is the filter and the modulation between voices; on the Super Nintendo, envelopes and echo; on the PC Engine, the
wavetable; on FM chips, the operators and how they are wired.

We read the chip's state roughly every millisecond of emulated sound, and each frame shows the latest reading, as is.
If a value jumps, the chip jumped.

\section{SID (Commodore 64)}
\capture[0.8]{sidcells-now-playing}{SID dashboard}
Each column is a voice. From top to bottom you read:
\begin{description}
  \item[Wave] the waveform (triangle, sawtooth, pulse, noise, or a combination) and, for the pulse, its duty cycle.
        When the duty cycle never stops moving, that is the ``PWM'' that gives Rob Hubbard's basses their weight.
  \item[Env] the envelope: the current level, the phase (attack, decay, sustain, release) and the ADSR times in
        milliseconds. A 2~ms attack and a short decay make a percussive note; a high sustain, a held note.
  \item[Freq] the nearest note and the frequency in hertz. An arpeggio shows here: the note changes every frame.
  \item[Mod] sync and ring modulation. We show only the source voice: ``Sync V3'', ``Ring V2'', ``Sync+Ring V1''. On
        the SID a voice is always modulated by the previous one (V1 by V3, V2 by V1, V3 by V2); knowing the source is
        enough.
\end{description}
The last row shows the master volume, resonance, filter type and the voices routed into it. If you see a voice going
in and out of the filter in time with the music, you have caught one of the era's favourite tricks.

\section{Super Nintendo (S-DSP)}
\capturedouble{snes}{snes-inspector}{View of the 8 voices}{voice inspector}
The S-DSP plays eight sample voices. For each you see the sample number, the note, the drawn envelope (ADSR or GAIN
mode, which many drivers use for their own fades), left and right volumes, and three flags: echo, noise, pitch
modulation.

At the bottom: master volumes, echo volume and feedback, and the eight coefficients of the FIR filter that colours
its repeats. High feedback with a low-pass FIR is the large reverberant hall of Square's games; a short, dry echo, a
dungeon mood. Tap a voice to open its inspector and see its envelope at full size.

\section{PC Engine (HuC6280)}
\capture[0.8]{pce-dashboard}{PC Engine view}
Each of the six voices loops over a table of 32 5-bit values: its waveform. Each voice's rectangle draws the table in
use, step by step. Next to it, a level bar and two pan bars, then the note, frequency and level.

We describe the shape in words: ``Sine'', ``Triangle'', ``Saw $\uparrow$'', ``Square'', ``Pulse 25\%''\dots{} and
we number each distinct table. When the number changes mid-track, the driver has just reloaded the voice with another
instrument. The HuC6280 cannot read a table backwards: a falling saw is a table the driver wrote in reverse, hence
the label ``Saw $\downarrow$ (reversed)''.

Each voice's bottom row gives its registers as written: \$802 and \$803 (frequency), \$804 (volume and enable),
\$805 (pan), \$806 (DDA mode), \$807 (noise). The global row gives \$801 (master volume), \$808 and \$809 (LFO). When
DDA mode is on, the voice is playing samples sent one by one by the processor: that is how the PC Engine makes its
characters speak.

\section{FM chips}
\capturedouble{fm-megadrive-cards}{fm-megadrive-inspector}{Mega Drive: voice cards}{voice inspector}
Each card shows a voice: its algorithm, drawn with carriers at the bottom and modulators above, and each operator's
level. Remember one simple rule: the stronger a modulator, the richer and harsher the sound. A Mega Drive bass that
``snaps'' has a modulator whose envelope drops very fast.

Tap a voice to open the inspector: each operator's envelope drawn large, frequency multipliers, detune, operator 1
feedback and LFO sensitivity.
\capturedouble{fmview-algorithms}{fm-adlib-cards}{FM algorithms}{AdLib}
For the AdLib (OPL2), each voice has only two operators and two possible connections: in series (one modulates the
other) or in parallel (both are heard). We lay out the nine voices 3 by 3.

\section{NES, MT-32 and PCM chips}
\capturedouble{nes}{mt32parts}{NES}{Roland MT-32: parts}
On the NES, the cards of the two square voices draw their duty cycle (12.5, 25, 50 or 75~\%) and give the 4-bit
volume, the envelope and the pitch sweep; the triangle and noise follow, then the state of the DMC channel, which
plays samples. For the MT-32 there are no useful registers to show: each part shows its instrument name, the keys
played and the state of its ``partials'', the synthesis building blocks the module combines into a sound.
\capturedouble{pcmchips-model1-portrait}{pcmchips-scsp-portrait}{YMW258 (Model 1/2)}{SCSP (Saturn, Model 2A/3)}
The PCM chips of Sega arcade boards and the Saturn play dozens of samples at once. Each cell shows an active voice:
its mode (PCM, FM or noise on the SCSP), its note, its pan and its total level (TL) in decibels.
